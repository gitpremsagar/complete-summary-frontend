<Section id="s3" title="The billionaire's brain: belief and main character energy">

<ul>
  <li>Then you <b>repeat</b> the story, and "you got to get obsessive about this." Every day: Who am I? What's possible?</li>
</ul>

<Section id="s4" title="Write your story: the Default Mode Network, priming and confirmation">
<p>Raj asks: what if someone has <b>never</b> believed in themselves, and never thought they could become better, reach their goals, or become financially free? How do they build main character energy?</p>
<KeyPoint><b>Answer: write the story down</b>"Take a piece of paper and... you don't have to believe it in the beginning. Just trust me." Write it as if it were written by <b>a version of you that doesn't exist yet</b>, one who believes in themselves, is successful and amazing. Write from that person's perspective. The content depends on your goals and vision.</KeyPoint>
<p><b>Her own example:</b> "I can come up with any scientific question related to the things that I am obsessed with and I will find the resources. I will find the people. I will find the funding. I will find the doors. I will make the doors open so that I can figure out what those answers are..." And she keeps telling herself that.</p>
<h3>The science: neural networks and the DMN</h3>
<ul>
  <li>A <b>neural network</b> is a pattern of electrical activation: a collection of brain areas that are connected electrically and activate together "in unison like an orchestra." The brain has many of them.</li>
  <li>An important one is the <b>DMN, the Default Mode Network</b>, which is "the brain's storyteller," its <b>narrator</b>. It handles <b>autobiographical narration</b>: who am I, what is my identity, what are my stories.</li>
  <li>When people talk about trauma and "baggage," they are talking about <b>stories</b> collected "in a bag" and carried around. Ask yourself <b>which stories you are still carrying and whether they serve you</b>. Do they help you reach your goals? Make you happier? Healthier? Give you the life you want? If not, "rip that story up" and write a new one.</li>
  <li>Review the new story <b>every day</b>. (To Raj, playfully: "You're going to get every guest you want.")</li>
  <li>The DMN's pattern of activation <b>influences other brain areas related to attention and executive function</b>. Your beliefs and stories shape what you see, what you pay attention to, and what you notice.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Priming: why you suddenly notice opportunities</h3>
<p>Priming means having information "teed up." That is why writing the story once and putting it in a journal isn't enough: "This is training. You got to hit the gym every single day." Keep writing and reviewing your goals, how you'll get there, and the plan.</p>
<ul>
  <li>What happens next is that <b>you start noticing opportunities</b>. "The opportunities are everywhere... but people don't see them. You will only see what the brain is primed to see."</li>
  <li>Success stories often include "then I met this person randomly," "one conversation," "the idea just came to me." She says it isn't that random: you can <b>increase the statistical likelihood</b> of these "random" events.</li>
  <li>Raj sums it up as "if you reset your DMN," and she loves the phrase: use the DMN to <b>design your beliefs</b> about yourself, what's possible, what you'll accomplish and who you are, and that will change your behavior.</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>Biological law: the brain looks for data that confirms its beliefs</b>This works for negative beliefs too. People who believe "I'm a victim, everybody hates me, I don't get what I want" keep finding evidence and keep complaining. "So choose your beliefs, design your beliefs." If a belief isn't helping you, "throw it away."</KeyPoint>
<h3>Raj's personal example</h3>
<p>Raj has an "irrational belief" about his work. On a random afternoon he spots opportunities "out of thin air" that turn into opportunities worth millions, things that weren't even real five days earlier. At the same time he plays the <b>victim card about sleep</b>: because of his life, schedule and work, he believes he's "never able to sleep properly" and "not this guy who'll ever be able to get sleep." Every little thing confirms it. Even with 10 hours available, he finds an excuse for why he didn't sleep. Same person: in business the opportunities keep coming, in health he "keeps falling."</p>

<Section id="s5" title="Identity-based beliefs: the template (applied to Raj's sleep)">

<h3>Step 1: Identity-based belief rooted in your values</h3>
<p>Write a belief about sleep that is based on your <b>values</b>. Example: <i>"I prioritize my health because I know how important it is for my performance and my business."</i> Does Raj believe it? "Absolutely."</p>
<h3>Step 2: Stack the data you know</h3>
<p><i>"I know the data on sleep and health."</i> Raj has had sleep experts on the podcast. "Still, it has not helped me. A lot of people have benefited from it, not me." She admits she isn't an easy sleeper either: "We can bond about this."</p>
<h3>Step 3: What gets better</h3>
<p>What does he know for sure about sleep? "It's going to make me better." She pushes it further: <i>"I could be a smarter, better-looking and happier version of myself if I slept a little bit more."</i> Raj adds: he'd ask better questions and be more focused, "so many things I'll achieve by just sleeping better."</p>
<h3>Step 4: The new habit, written as if it's already true</h3>
<p>Bring it all together into an identity-based belief, <b>even though it's not true yet</b>: <i>"I make sure that I wind down for bed at an early enough time to make sure I at least get 6 to 7 hours a night."</i> This is where you're <b>designing the habit</b>.</p>
<p>Raj laughs: "This is the part I don't believe." She says that's the process of <b>"fake it till you make it."</b> "You got to be like, yep, I do that," because you prioritize your health and performance.</p>
<KeyPoint><b>Raj's recap of the template</b>1) Identity (values) &rarr; 2) Data &rarr; 3) What better will happen &rarr; 4) New habit, combined into one full sentence.</KeyPoint>
<p><b>Example from one of her MBA students</b> (note the "type of person" framing, which contrasts you with someone else, such as a competitor):</p>

<Section id="s6" title="Implementation intentions: when-then plans">
<p>Once the identity belief is written ("I'm the type of person who does this"), the next step is <b>implementation intentions</b>, from research by <b>Peter Gollwitzer</b>. They are <b>situation-action plans</b>: like an old-school <b>if-then</b> statement in computer science, though <b>"when-then"</b> is the more apt description.</p>
<Quote cite="Dr Yousef's example situation-action plan">When my alarm goes off every night at 11 p.m., I begin my designed routine. I start my wind-down music playlist, then I make my herbal tea. I do this. I start doing my physical therapy... whatever it may be.</Quote>
<h3>Why it beats "I'll try to go to bed earlier"</h3>
<ul>
  <li>An implementation intention describes, <b>"in crazy detail," what it looks like when you do the thing</b>, like a movie. "I want to see you in your house, Raj... it's 11 p.m., the alarm goes off. Where are you? What are you wearing? Where in the house are you? What are you doing? Phone's in your hand?"</li>
  <li>A goal without a plan "is like a prayer... It's cute. It's nice, but there's no plan." When people say "I'm going to try to go to sleep earlier," her reaction is: "Adorable. How are you going to do that?"</li>
  <li>For most people (unless their body wakes them super early), more sleep means <b>going to bed earlier</b>, so design an implementation intention that gets you there.</li>
  <li>Raj tries it: "When my alarm goes off around 11 p.m., I'm on the right side of the bed, my laptop is on, I'll shut it down..." She confirms: yes, that level of minute detail.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Tips for making it stick</h3>
<ol>
  <li><b>Visualize it and write it down</b>, then attempt the habit.</li>
  <li><b>Make it as easy and small as possible.</b> Many people go to bed earlier simply by <b>brushing their teeth earlier</b>. "I don't know when I'm going to go to sleep, but I told myself I have to brush my teeth at 11:00." Once you've brushed, you probably won't eat or drink anymore, so the kitchen is cut off. Then "I'm going to get in my pajamas," which is one step closer. Figure out which behaviors you want to design out.</li>
  <li><b>Commit</b>, and make the <b>"when" part (the situation, the trigger) as clear as humanly possible.</b> People really skip this step.</li>
</ol>
<h3>Implementation intentions for bad habits</h3>
<p>For cutting a habit (smoking, sweets, late-night reels, which Raj calls "a tricky one"): <i>"When I have a craving for another piece of chocolate / a cigarette, then I will do this instead."</i> That is a version for a <b>negative behavior</b>, one you want to remove rather than add.</p>
<Warn><b>Important</b>Removing a behavior is "insanely difficult... way more than adding." Raj asks why, and she heads to the whiteboard (next section).</Warn>

<Section id="s7" title="Why breaking a habit is harder than building one (the whiteboard)">
<p>Back to neural networks as patterns of electrical activation. On the whiteboard she draws a dot representing the <b>craving</b> (Raj picks mindless scrolling and binge-watching reels; she draws a little phone). The craving connects to another set of activation patterns representing the <b>complex behavior</b>: grabbing the phone and scrolling.</p>
<h3>The craving is really a feeling</h3>
<ul>
  <li>The craving usually isn't really <i>for</i> binge-watching. Binge-watching is the behavior that satisfies some <b>feeling</b>: something you're missing, or something you want to "disassociate from, numb from."</li>
  <li>The usual feelings: <b>overwhelm, stress, tiredness, lack of motivation</b>. Nobody wants to feel these, so the brain says "let's get out of this. How do I solve this?"</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>Habit = addiction = a behavior you do a lot</b>People hear "addiction" and think of something scary or moral. But people are addicted to exercise, to feeling healthy, to being kind, to their own success (the kind of people Raj "collects"). "Addiction just means habit." Demystify and "dethrone" the words: habits, addictions and behaviors you repeat a lot are the same thing.</KeyPoint>
<h3>The loop, step by step</h3>
<ol>
  <li>You have a feeling, some cognitive or emotional brain state, and want to move away from it. The brain doesn't want to let you sit in a bad feeling.</li>
  <li>One time, you binge-watched while feeling that way ("let me distract myself"). At first, being distracted <b>feels good</b>.</li>
  <li>Example: you're overwhelmed about a work deadline and scroll for <b>45 minutes</b> in the evening instead of working on it.</li>
  <li>You put the phone down. Did you deal with the thing stressing you? No. Raj: "I get guilty."</li>
  <li>Now you have <b>overwhelm plus shame</b>, which is worse than before, so the brain wants to go around the circle again.</li>
  <li>Be honest: most people have done this "at least 100 times."</li>
</ol>
<p>Every time you traverse this road, the pathway gets "darker... bigger, stronger, deeper." The network becomes "like a tree trunk, like the roots of a tree." Do it once and it's fine; do it 100, 1,000 or a million times and it becomes a <b>really entrenched and strong association between a feeling, a craving and a behavior</b>.</p>
<p>That's why "next time I'm exhausted, I will not binge watch" is "impossible. Good luck." Because the network is so deep, "It's going to happen automatically... Raj is not driving this car."</p>
</Section>

<Section id="s8" title="How to break a bad habit: cognitive pause and replacement behavior">
<p><b>Tip number one:</b> ideally, don't wait until the habit gets that strong. But assuming it already is (Raj: "there must be hundreds of bad behaviors which are strong in my head... let's get rid of them first"):</p>
<h3>Step 1: Awareness</h3>
<p>You have to see the loop for yourself. "You cannot change something that you do not understand, that you don't have data on." You need <b>self-knowledge</b>, the ability to see your own behavior as it happens. So you must identify <b>the emotions and feelings that start the loop</b>.</p>
<p>Raj asks how to identify the feeling behind cravings like midnight dessert, binge-watching, alcohol, weed, cigarettes or porn, when it could be overwhelm, stress, anxiety, tiredness, shame, feeling unable to turn your life around, or feeling like a victim.</p>
<KeyPoint><b>The cognitive pause</b>Before you grab the chocolate at midnight, or even after you've opened Instagram and are about to scroll, stop: "Hold on, hold on. What am I feeling right now? Why do I want to go and binge watch right now?" You aren't forbidden from doing it. "In the beginning, we just want to know why."</KeyPoint>
<p>Raj answers: "I'm just exhausted." She says go deeper: <b>why</b> are you exhausted? And if you're exhausted, shouldn't you <b>rest</b> or do something that gives you energy? Right before bed, the answer is to go to sleep. In the middle of the day, "I'm exhausted and I need a break" is not a reason to scroll, because <b>scrolling is not a break</b>.</p>

<Study>
<h4>Which breaks actually restore you? (paper in <i>Nature</i>, 2024)</h4>
<dl>
  <dt>Setup</dt><dd>Compared break types: (1) <b>no break at all</b> ("keep working... hustle"), (2) looking outside or <b>taking a walk in nature</b>, (3) <b>scrolling</b>.</dd>
  <dt>Finding</dt><dd><b>Scrolling was essentially the same as taking no break</b>. It doesn't let the brain rest and replenish energy.</dd>
</dl>
</Study>

<p><b>Treat yourself like an Olympic athlete.</b> When an athlete says "I'm exhausted," we tell them to get off the field, sit, drink water, have a snack and stretch, so they can go back out and keep training. Nobody tells them to open TikTok. Science already shows that isn't a break.</p>
<h3>Step 2: A tiny replacement behavior</h3>
<p>You can't just say "I won't do it." You need a <b>replacement behavior</b>, and you must intervene where the loop <b>starts: the feeling</b>. Raj picks <b>breath work</b>. "Like just 1 minute." She agrees: <b>60 seconds, close your eyes</b>, nothing else.</p>
<ul>
  <li>Her escape hatch for the truly addicted: "If after 60 seconds of closing your eyes you still want to scroll, go for it." That makes it far easier to commit to.</li>
  <li>The energy now flows <b>feeling &rarr; close eyes</b> before it can reach scrolling. "You're breaking the cycle." The first time you might still scroll; the second time, maybe you do something else.</li>
  <li>Raj: if I do this every time, will it reduce? "If you do it every time, I promise you're going to start not doing it." At worst you become "that guy who closes his eyes for 60 seconds before he binge-watches," which is a new route that still gives the brain <b>an opportunity to redirect</b>.</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>What you are actually training: emotional regulation</b>"Every behavior, even people doing hard drugs, porn addiction, it's coming from an emotion... If you can understand the emotion, you can control the behavior, because you can choose it." Regulating is hard, so create a cognitive pause plus something tiny and easy: a <b>pattern interrupt</b> and a chance to choose differently.</KeyPoint>
<p>Over time, after <b>1 month</b> the old road gets skinnier, after <b>3 months</b> skinnier still, while the new road keeps getting stronger. "This is how you build new habits and kill bad ones."</p>
<h3>Why killing a habit is harder than starting one</h3>
<p>You are doing <b>two jobs at once</b>: <b>pruning</b> (deactivating and weakening an existing network) <i>and</i> building a new one. Business analogy: it's harder to <b>downsize one department and create a new one</b> than to simply say "everyone keep going, we're also starting a new project." Pruning is hard because energy wants to flow the way you've let it flow over and over; that "muscle became really strong."</p>
<p>The same goes for <b>identity stories</b>. If you keep saying "I'm a victim, I'll stay poor forever, the system is messed up, successful people got there through tricks or luck, my family isn't lucky, that doesn't happen for people from where I'm from," you reinforce it: "This is real. This is real." <b>Beliefs look for data and data informs beliefs</b>, and the cycle keeps strengthening until it "becomes your whole life."</p>

<Section id="s9" title="Bad digital hygiene and the UT Austin phone study">
<p>Raj asks what she teaches top executives, CEOs and multi-millionaires that an average 25 or 35-year-old could also use. She says the <b>first and most pervasive issue</b> she sees in people's lives and workplaces, as it relates to productivity, is <b>bad digital hygiene</b>.</p>
<KeyPoint><b>Definition</b>Like hygiene (clean hands), digital hygiene is your relationship with your digital tools, <b>especially notifications</b>. Bad digital hygiene means a bad relationship with them.</KeyPoint>
<p>When you sit down to focus, where's your phone? Raj: "It's on my desk, like right here." So she runs a mini-experiment, putting Raj in the shoes of a participant in a <b>UT Austin study</b>.</p>
<h3>What was measured</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>General fluid intelligence</b>: "your mind's ability to hold important information in mind and then get new information and compute with that information without losing the stuff that was already in your mind." Raj has been doing this all conversation ("That's why you're successful"). Some people lose track of what they were holding and can't compute the new information.</li>
  <li>It is measured with a <b>complex working memory task</b>, "a phenomenal way to test for general fluid intelligence." <b>Attention span</b> was tested too.</li>
</ul>

<Study>
<h4>Phone proximity and brain power (University of Texas at Austin)</h4>
<dl>
  <dt>Setup</dt><dd>Every participant turned their phone <b>completely off</b> (not DND, not airplane mode, powered off). Then:<br />
  <b>Scenario 1:</b> the phone is in <b>another room</b> (the waiting room, held by the researcher).<br />
  <b>Scenario 2:</b> the phone is in your <b>pocket or bag</b>.<br />
  <b>Scenario 3:</b> the phone is <b>face down on the desk</b>, visible off to the side, exactly where Raj keeps his.<br />
  In all three the phone is dead, so technically nothing changes. "It's like having this eraser nearby."</dd>
  <dt>Finding</dt><dd>When the phone was <b>visible</b>, even dead and face down, <b>scores were significantly decreased across the board</b>: attention span <i>and</i> general fluid intelligence. "That means you're dumber. Literally." Same Raj, same brain, different capability. She calls it "one of the most shocking studies I think I have seen in the last number of years on this topic."</dd>
</dl>
</Study>

<h3>Why a dead phone still drains you</h3>
<p>The brain is all about storytelling and narration. All your memories tied to the phone (binge-watching, friends, plans, good news, bad news, drama, Apple Pay, pictures of her dog) are in there. "Your brain knows that. So if you can see it, it's always going to be thinking... I want to play." Raj admits that ever since they started talking about the phone, he's been anticipating a notification, even though it's dead.</p>
<h3>Her recommendations</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Have a <b>"parking lot," a box, somewhere to hide your phone</b>. "Out of sight, out of mind."</li>

<h3>Can anyone be a good multitasker?</h3>
<p>First, define terms. When people say "multitasking," they mean doing two different things <b>with full fidelity at the same time</b>. But "How many brains do you have?" One. One attention system. "You don't have two 100s. You have one 100." You can slice your 100% like a pie chart (50/50, 80/20, 70/30, 60/40), but whatever the split, <b>your attention is split</b>.</p>
<ul>
  <li>People say "my phone is here but I'm not looking; my email is open but I'm focused." But when a notification pops up, "I see you go. You look."</li>
  <li>On Zoom calls you can see people's eyes drop and come back up. "We can all see you... Nobody's that slick." Everyone thinks they can spot others doing it but nobody can tell when <i>they</i> do it. Wrong.</li>
  <li>"It's not your choice to pay attention or not pay attention to distractions when they come up." The brain is a <b>monotasking machine</b>, not a multitasking machine.</li>
</ul>
<h3>The nuance: yes, you can do two things, but know the math</h3>
<p>She listens to podcasts and calls her mother while driving. (Her mom complains about her knee, her back, "you need to come home," "always the same stories.") But as a neuroscientist she admits she is <b>not 100% focused on the road</b>, or she wouldn't hear her mom at all. It works because she knows the route in her hometown, which is essentially <b>autopilot</b>. So: you can do two things, but "you just have to know the math... understand the biological laws... be strategic about it."</p>
<KeyPoint><b>What actually happens: task switching (context switching)</b>What Raj did wasn't multitasking, which is impossible as defined. It was <b>task switching</b>, which everyone does all the time. Every switch (A to B, B to C, to the Apple Watch, the phone, back to the document, "someone came into my office, what did you want?") carries a <b>biological payment</b>: <b>time</b> (more than double in Raj's case, "and time is money") and <b>energy</b> (you exhaust yourself faster).</KeyPoint>
<p>And this test was <i>easy</i>. "Whose job is this easy? You want to build an empire... a company... cure cancer. Nothing is this easy." When you're doing something hard, "respect yourself enough to put the distractions out of sight, out of mind: notifications off, no email open, no phone next to you." At first it will be painful and your mind will be all over the place, "but you just need to get your muscles strong again."</p>

<Section id="s14" title="Ideas: more ideas, not better ideas. Be bored (DMN vs CEN)">

<h3>DMN vs CEN</h3>
<Grid cols={2}>
  <Box><h4>DMN: Default Mode Network</h4>The storyteller: narration, stories, daydreaming, boredom, mind wandering. When it's active, ideas "float to the surface."</Box>

  <li>Raj's term: <b>"bloom scrolling"</b>, the opposite of doom scrolling, where you curate your feed so it makes you better. She loves it, but adds: bloom or doom, you still need to put it away sometimes and "give your mind space to breathe."</li>

<Section id="s15" title="The science of shower thoughts">
<p>Raj has had "so many" great ideas in the shower. Here's why it happens:</p>
<ol>
  <li>When you think about a problem, a certain pattern of electrical activation (neural networks across brain areas) is running. Picture it as a <b>gridlock</b>: your brain is occupied with, and stuck on, the problem.</li>
  <li>To find a solution, you need to "move these networks around" and get <b>different electrical activity</b>.</li>
</ol>
<KeyPoint><b>The method she teaches business leaders</b>1) <b>Load up</b> all the data and information about the problem: think about it, understand it, review everything. 2) Then <b>walk away</b>. When you're stuck on "What's the solution? I don't know," get up and do something else. 3) Doing something else <b>introduces a new variable</b>: drink water ("seriously, something as small as this"), a different playlist, a different room. Anything you touch, smell, drink or listen to.</KeyPoint>
<ul>
  <li>Your desk is full of associations with the problem, so <b>change the environment</b>.</li>
  <li>In the shower or on a walk, the problem networks are still active (or recently active), and you're adding new variables. <b>You're not scrolling</b>, which is key: if you distract yourself with texting people back and WhatsApp, "you're not going to solve any problems because now you're just doing more stuff."</li>
  <li>"You have to not do stuff to give your brain space to say, 'Wait a minute, light bulb.'" You've loosened your grip and introduced new data.</li>
  <li>Good options: a walk, cooking dinner, exercise, a run. But <b>not something so immersive</b> that you can't keep the problem in the background, so don't sit down and watch a new movie.</li>
  <li>Exception: <b>a rerun you've seen 1,000 times</b>, like an old sitcom. It's soothing because you're not really paying attention and your mind wanders. (Neuroscience explains many everyday experiences like this.)</li>
</ul>
<Warn><b>Why scrolling kills this</b>Scrolling is "all novel." And it is "designed by people like me, by the way, cognitive neuroscientists... hired by these companies. We use everything we know about how to capture your attention and keep your attention to make money off of you."</Warn>
</Section>

<Section id="s16" title="Negative news, threat bias and losing your inner voice">

<p><b>Raj's point:</b> since evolution began, the most primitive human feeling is <b>threat to survival</b>. Induce threat and people get anxious, and anxious people keep consuming. So much of the world's news taps that one emotion: everything is wrong, everyone is against you, tomorrow things will go down. That's why positive content is harder to grow; "we can make this go 10x more viral if we start just making it about threats and anxieties." She agrees: "It's so sad... But it is how the brain works."</p>
<ul>
  <li>This is another biological law: "You can ignore it, but other companies know it and they're using it against you." So learn the laws.</li>
  <li>We can't change our biology here (thousands of years of evolution), but <b>you can cut yourself off</b>.</li>
</ul>
<h3>The executive news cleanse</h3>
<p>She has worked with high-powered executives who needed serious <b>content cleanses</b>. For some in a dark, foggy place, she cut them off the news entirely for a while. What she tells them:</p>
<Quote cite="Dr Sahar Yousef">The world's going to go on whether you're looking at it or not. You're not that important... I know you're a fancy CEO running a company, but you're not deciding on what's happening with the war.</Quote>

<KeyPoint><b>A big issue she sees: no inner voice</b>People complain they can't hear their own thoughts anymore; there's <b>no internal narration</b>. But that's where ideas come from. "It's coming from the inside... You can use the outside stuff to trigger some ideas, but ideas are still coming from inside." <Section id="s19" title="Why short-form makes us lonelier: dopamine, oxytocin and serotonin">
<p>Raj asks what in the brain makes short-form consumers lonelier. She says scientists aren't 100% sure yet, but gives her <b>hypothesis</b> as someone currently studying it.</p>
<ul>
  <li>There's a <b>loneliness epidemic</b>, not just in the US but in pretty much any part of the world that has embraced technology.</li>
  <li>It's paradoxical: technology is amazing at connecting people (she and Raj's team connected from halfway across the world). "I'm not anti-tech." So how can we be more connected yet more lonely?</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>Biological law: humans are pack animals</b>"It's not your choice." We're designed for a collective, not to live like lone wolves in silos. <b>Human connection is a need like food or water.</b> "You'll get sick if you don't get it. And that's been proven."</KeyPoint>
<p><b>Example:</b> long-haul truck drivers, who have nobody to talk to for days or weeks, show brain degradation and atrophy, "almost like accelerated aging or dementia," and many of them are young men.</p>
<h3>The craving for connection and two ways to answer it</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Real connection:</b> in real life is best (friends, the people you live with, family). Next best is a phone call or FaceTime, then at least a text.</li>
  <li><b>Social media:</b> "Let's be honest, social media is not social anymore." When Instagram started you saw posts from your friends; now it's mostly <b>people you don't know</b>. You're distracted, and distraction feels good in the moment.</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>Analogy: candy when you're hungry</b>You're hungry for real food, you eat candy instead, and an hour later you're even hungrier because you never got nutrition. Seeking connection through short-form scrolling is candy: a little lonely before, even lonelier after, because "it never satisfies that need."</KeyPoint>
<h3>The neurochemistry (her hypothesis)</h3>

<thead>
<tr><th>Chemical</th><th>Role in this loop</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
  <tr><td><b>Dopamine</b></td><td>"The brain's primary motivation molecule." It drives your desire and motivation toward social connection.</td></tr>

<p>Choose <b>option A</b>, grabbing the phone and scrolling, and you're not bonding with anyone ("I don't even know them"), so there's no oxytocin, no contentment, and more anxiety and depressive symptoms. Choose <b>option B</b>, grabbing a person, and you get oxytocin and serotonin, so you "check the box." Dopamine motivated you, you sought connection, and you got it.</p>

<h3>How the team works together</h3>
<ol>
  <li><b>Story Studios = the DMN</b>, "where all the stories are made... the story-making machine." It holds Raj's story ("I want to be this podcaster") and how he'll get there. It sends that information to City Hall. <b>This is where meaning comes from</b>: why am I doing this?</li>

  <li><b>The Watchtower = the DAN, the Dorsal Attention Network</b>. Like the "Eye of Sauron" in <i>Lord of the Rings</i> (Raj hasn't seen it), it points a <b>spotlight</b> of attention wherever it's told. The tricky part is <i>who</i> tells it where to shine. City Hall can, but so can others:</li>
  <li><b>The Alarm Tower = the amygdala</b>, a walnut-sized area deep in the center of the brain: "the brain's alarm system." In an emergency ("oh my god, panic") the Watchtower gets told "Raj, stop focusing on your Google Doc... go look at this."</li>
  <li><b>The Dispatch Center = the salience network</b>, "like emergency calls, your satellite system." Something's happening; maybe it's not an emergency, but it redirects attention anyway.</li>
</ol>
<p>This "amazing team" will either help you meet your goals or make it harder, <b>depending on how you set yourself up</b>. With a clean setup, meaning flows from the story, City Hall focuses, the Watchtower points at the research, and as long as nothing interrupts you, you keep focusing.</p>
<p>But the usual setup, as Raj describes it: "one notification, another... five tabs open, two people talking... I have to message... probably scroll a little in between." Every notification makes the salience network say "Hey, look over here," and the Watchtower obeys. The salience network is essentially the <b>external distraction</b> system, telling you where to orient attention.</p>
<KeyPoint><b>You can't shut it down, so control your environment</b>The salience network is "there to keep you alive." Like gravity, you can't fight it. What you can do is <b>clean and control your environment</b> so the whole system is set up for success. That's why digital hygiene matters.</KeyPoint>
<h3>The exam-room analogy</h3>
<p>Imagine your child studied hard but came home with a bad grade, and you learned the teacher let everyone use phones, play music, chat and walk in and out during the exam, "like a party." Every parent would go crazy and demand a redo: "This is not how you give an exam." <b>So how do you sit down to work?</b> As leaders (she runs a team too), ask: is your team's environment built for focus or for distraction?</p>
<h3>The Library &amp; Archive: hippocampus and memory</h3>
<ul>
  <li>The <b>hippocampus</b> is your memory store. It sits physically close to the <b>amygdala</b>, the seat of fear, anxiety and negative affect.</li>
  <li><b>Negative and scary things are more memorable.</b> This is true for all emotion: heighten your emotions and you remember the situation better. Even <b>being afraid of forgetting something makes you less likely to forget it</b>.</li>
  <li><b>How to improve memory in general</b> (a counterintuitive fun fact): work on your <b>attention</b>. "You can only remember what you're attending to." Sharpen your focus and memory improves. Attention is the <b>funnel</b>: things have to come in before they can go down into memory.</li>
</ul>

<h3>The three chronotypes</h3>

<Section id="s24" title="Burnout vs stress: completing the stress cycle">
<p>Raj asks how burnout differs from anxiety or stress. Isn't burnout just what happens when you're stressed and anxious all the time?</p>
<p>"The answer is no. They're not the same thing." But yes, if you keep stressing over and over, you're likely to burn out at some point, depending on <b>how you experience that stress</b>.</p>
<h3>Stress isn't the villain</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Humans are <b>designed to experience stress</b>. These days "stress" is treated like a naughty word, with everything about "anti-stress." But stress just means stress hormones, which bring <b>focus, alertness and motivation</b>.</li>
  <li>"If you removed all the cortisol in your body, you would be in a <b>coma</b>." Cortisol isn't bad; you need it.</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>The stress cycle</b>Cortisol goes up, and "what goes up must come down." Get stressed about something, deal with it, and it's done. You can go through <b>dozens of complete stress cycles a day</b> and be fine.</KeyPoint>
<h3>What modern life looks like</h3>
<p>The alarm goes off, you grab the phone (it's the alarm), scroll emails, messages and WhatsApp, fire off "yes, confirmed, thumbs up, heart," check the news, all <b>before</b> you've listened to your inner voice (priorities, goals, who you are, your story, your mindset) or done any work on yourself. "You've launched yourself onto this roller coaster ride that you didn't anticipate" and started the stress response. Then it continues: work, first meeting, next meeting, more emails, calls... It never comes down.</p>
<Quote cite="Dr Sahar Yousef">Stress doesn't kill. Never completing the stress cycle kills.</Quote>
<p>If you never close the cycle, stress keeps rising; you pass out, wake up and repeat. Eventually you burn out: your adrenals "will finally give out" and say "I'm done giving you cortisol."</p>
<ul>
  <li>"Can you imagine going back and telling your grandfather 'I'm burned out' at your age?" Why is everyone suddenly burning out?</li>
  <li>A friend of hers supervises a <b>22-and-a-half-year-old</b> fresh graduate who, one quarter into his first job, said he was clinically burned out and needed a doctor. "You just started... How is that even feasible?"</li>
  <li>She doesn't think most people claiming burnout actually have it. <b>Clinical burnout is super serious.</b> Being stressed and anxious isn't the same thing, and ordinary stress won't kill you. But <b>chronic stress that never closes, over a long time</b>, can harm your health. The goal: <b>close out your stress cycles</b>.</li>
</ul>
</Section>

Mentally off.</KeyPoint>

<Section id="s26" title="The unpublished 9-week Berkeley study (450 students)">
<p>Raj asks about her recent research on stressed, anxious, lonely and depressed people. She says it's <b>unpublished</b>, Raj is "the first to hear about this," and she was looking at the data the night before: "I'm very excited."</p>

<Study tag="Unpublished study">
<h4>"Mind, Machine and Meaning": a class that is a living experiment</h4>
<dl>
  <dt>Who</dt><dd>A UC Berkeley undergraduate class of about <b>450 students</b>.</dd>
  <dt>Idea</dt><dd>Instead of only learning, students must <b>change their behavior</b>, breaking habits and building new ones, so they go through <b>behavior change and neuroplasticity</b> during the class. "Hard work." Everything is tracked.</dd>
  <dt>Baseline</dt><dd>Students volunteered ("sign up for science") and came to the lab to test <b>attention span</b>, <b>complex working memory</b>, <b>loneliness</b>, <b>meaning and life satisfaction</b>, <b>depression</b>, <b>anxiety</b> and <b>stress</b>.</dd>
  <dt>Intervention</dt><dd>A <b>9-week digital detox</b> (details below).</dd>
  <dt>Results</dt><dd>After 9 weeks: <b>significantly less lonely, significantly less depressed, significantly less anxious</b>. <b>Attention spans rose significantly</b>, and <b>complex working memory</b> (a proxy for general fluid intelligence) <b>increased significantly</b>. "It's almost unbelievable... I didn't think it was going to be this good... this many benefits so fast."</dd>
</dl>
</Study>

<h3>What the 9-week detox involved</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Changing their relationship with scrolling</b>, specifically short-form and social media. Long-form was fine.</li>
  <li><b>Homework:</b> one week, everyone had to watch a <b>movie of at least 90 minutes with no second screen</b> (Raj's podcast would count too). Raj: "What a privileged class." She: it's genuinely hard now. Many young people can't sit through a 90-minute movie, even a good one. "It's not their fault."</li>
  <li><b>Analogy (mapo tofu vs candy):</b> Raj's favorite meal is mapo tofu, which is nutritious and filling. If candy were always in your pocket and you got addicted, would you still like mapo tofu? "Not at all." That's why a movie feels like hard work to them.</li>
  <li><b>Limits on scrolling</b> with <b>social accountability</b>: go over the limit and a text goes to one of their friends, and she finds out too.</li>
  <li><b>Emotional regulation support:</b> for most people scrolling is a <b>coping mechanism</b> for stress (numbing). Take it away and they have to sit with the bad feelings, which feels awful. So a <b>therapist</b> taught healthier emotional regulation techniques.</li>
  <li><b>Stories:</b> people who had been through serious digital addiction came to share their journeys, for inspiration.</li>
  <li><b>Content diet / bloom scrolling:</b> unfollow accounts that don't teach, challenge or expose you to new ideas. Students researched and shared accounts that would "level you up" in their niche (art, business, and so on). "Go find the bloom. Seek the bloom."</li>
  <li><b>Less screen time overall</b>, replacing digital connection with <b>real in-person connection</b>: a huge party with food, games and live music, group hikes, and even <b>blind dates</b> she set up. ("What kind of class is this?" "The best class.")</li>
  <li><b>Final project:</b> everyone had to pick up <b>a hobby</b> and work on it all semester.</li>
</ul>
<ul>
  <li>It was hard: "a lot of tears... It's an addiction. Everyone is going through withdrawal together" (450 people at once), softened by "injecting connection and play."</li>
  <li>They <b>kept their phones</b> and still scrolled, just less. Someone at 2 hours a day might drop to about 30 minutes; some went from <b>6 hours to about 2</b>.</li>
  <li><b>Tougher conditions at the end:</b> baseline testing happened right after break, when everyone felt good. The post-test, 9 weeks later, fell <b>in the middle of midterms</b>, when everyone was very stressed. The results were still "that uplifting and inspiring."</li>
</ul>
<h3>Raj: "Wouldn't the results be biased?"</h3>
<p>Wouldn't students just give "good" survey answers after 9 weeks? Her response:</p>
<ul>
  <li><b>You can't game attention span.</b> The cognitive tests are digital assessments (for example, "Can you focus on this boring thing? How long before you make a mistake and miss something?"). She isn't asking anything; she's measuring.</li>
  <li>Self-report surveys (like loneliness) could in theory be lied on, but they were <b>anonymous</b>, so there was no benefit. Her bigger worry was stressed students rushing through without reading. She didn't want students to feel they had to please the professor.</li>
  <li>She offers to test Raj at her Berkeley lab: complex working memory, visual memory, attention span, "the whole thing."</li>
</ul>
<KeyPoint><b>Her conclusion</b>"People, especially young people, do not have to be as isolated and lonely, as depressed and anxious, and their attention spans do not need to be as shot as they are. <b>Neuroplasticity is real.</b>" Whatever we did to cause this, "you can undo it." Like muscles: eat badly and never move and something happens to your body; eat well and exercise and something else happens.</KeyPoint>
